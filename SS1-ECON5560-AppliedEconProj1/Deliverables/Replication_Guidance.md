## Part 2 — Replication Memo Guidance


**What this is.** A **five-page memo** (single-spaced, ~2,500 words, excluding references and any tables/figures) that reports your team's replication attempt and turns it into recommendations for a local (or non-local NGO). Think **policy brief with scholarly grounding**, not a lab report: it should read cleanly for a smart non-specialist, but be anchored in the actual literature.


This is like a hybrid between replication reports and policy briefs. Here are some examples to help you: 



Replication report → this is how to document your attempt and appraise the methods honestly. Notice they're upfront about what didn't work. Borrow how they narrate the process. Examples: 

- Can read several from this list: [https://ideas.repec.org/s/zbw/i4rdps2.html](https://ideas.repec.org/s/zbw/i4rdps2.html)

- One example: [https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/270744/1/I4R-DP027.pdf](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/270744/1/I4R-DP027.pdf)




Fed/policy brief → this is what your recommendations section should sound like: plain language, one clear takeaway up front, written for someone who isn't an economist. Borrow the tone and structure, not the topic. Examples:  

- **"More Autonomy for Frontline Workers Supports Higher-Paying Jobs"** (Nelson, Wilmers & Zhang, Aug 2024) [https://research.upjohn.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1067&context=up_policybriefs](https://research.upjohn.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1067&context=up_policybriefs)

- **"How State Governments Can Help Distressed Places"** (Bartik, June 2022)[https://research.upjohn.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1044&context=up_policybriefs](https://research.upjohn.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1044&context=up_policybriefs)






*(these are longer and more polished than what we expect — look at the structure and voice, not the length or the level of success.)*


**Reproduction is not graded.** You are graded on how clearly you document what you tried, how sound your appraisal is, and how useful your recommendations are — *not* on whether your numbers matched the paper's. A team that hit a wall and explained it well can earn full marks.


**Required sections** (roughly one page each):



- **The problem & the paper.** What real-world question does this study address, and what did it find? State the paper's headline result plainly. (½ page.)

- **What we did.** Your replication attempt: what data you used, what method, what you were able to reproduce and what you weren't. Be specific and honest about where it broke down if it did. (1 page.)

- **Appraisal — methods/academic audience.** A critical read: do the paper's methods support its claims? What are its limitations, assumptions, or weaknesses? This is where you engage the **literature** — situate the paper against related work, cite studies that agree, disagree, or use different approaches. (1–1½ pages.)

- **Recommendations — real-world audience.** Translate the finding for a non-technical stakeholder (e.g., a local organization). What should they take from this (the paper itself and any doubts, critiques, or new findings from the replication), and what should they do? Plain language, no jargon. What would you tell them to be cautious about? (1 page.)

- **References.** Properly formatted citations (see below). Not counted in the page limit.



**Literature referencing (this matters for your grade).**



- Cite **at least 5 scholarly sources** beyond the paper you're replicating — related studies, methods references, or work the paper itself builds on or contradicts.

- Use a **consistent citation style** (APA or Chicago author-date — pick one and stick to it). In-text citations + a reference list.

- Cite **specific claims**, not vibes. "Stated-preference methods are known to overstate WTP (Author, Year)" — not "many people think surveys are unreliable."

- **AI + citations — a warning.** If you use AI to help find or summarize literature, **verify every citation exists and says what you claim.** AI invents plausible-looking references that don't exist ("hallucinated" citations). A fake or misattributed citation is an academic-integrity problem, not a small slip. Check every one against the actual source.



**AI-use disclosure.** Per your team charter, include a short note (1–2 sentences) on how your team used AI in this project. This is expected and fine — undisclosed use is the problem.


**Format.** Five pages, single-spaced, 11–12pt, 1-inch margins. Tables/figures and references don't count toward the five pages. One memo per team; include your attribution list (who did what).



## Part 3 — Presentation Guidance


**What this is.** A short in-class presentation (target **~6-8 minutes + questions**) where your team presents your replication and its appraisal/recommendations. Built around a **two-slide appraisal core** — you can have a few framing slides around it, but the appraisal itself should fit on about two slides.


**What to cover** (a clear arc):



- **The paper & question** — what it studied, what it found (brief).

- **What you attempted & found** — your replication, honestly, including where it broke.

- **Appraisal** — do the methods hold up? (the academic read, condensed)

- **Recommendations** — what a real stakeholder should take from it. Land here — this is the payoff.



**Presentation norms (these are graded):**



- **Don't read your slides.** Slides support you; they're not your script. If you're reading, the audience is reading ahead of you.

- **No written aids that you look at, either**

- **Few words per slide.** Visual over textual. A slide crammed with text is a slide nobody reads and everybody tunes out.

- **Whole team engaged.** Smooth handoffs; everyone has a role. Don't have one person present while three stand silent.

- **Handle questions credibly** — it's fine to say "we're not sure, but here's our best thinking." Defensiveness reads worse than honest uncertainty.



**Timing.** Rehearse to length. Running long or short both signal under-preparation. Practice the handoffs specifically — that's where unrehearsed teams stumble.
