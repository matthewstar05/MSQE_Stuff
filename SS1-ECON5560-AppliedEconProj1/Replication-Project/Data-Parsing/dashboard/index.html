Does putting a dollar figure on it change the decision?

We rebuilt the main results of a choice experiment with New Zealand councillors, asking whether showing dollar values for some impacts of a development tilts decisions toward those impacts. Every number below comes from the authors' own data file, re-estimated by us.

Paper: Eppink, F. V., Winden, M., Wright, W. C. C., & Greenhalgh, S. (2016). Non-market values in a cost-benefit world: Evidence from a choice experiment. PLoS ONE, 11(10), e0165365. doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0165365

The question

Some impacts come with a price tag. Others don't.

When a council decides whether to rezone farmland for housing, the benefits arrive with a number attached: new rates revenue for the district. The costs often don't: a murkier stream, or a historic tavern that loses its open views.

The authors ran an online survey of the people who actually make these calls: regional and district councillors and council staff in New Zealand. Each person imagined a town of 20,000 growing by 4% a year and picked between two development plans and "keep things as they are", four times over.

The key move: people were randomly split into three groups that saw the same plans with different information. Because the split was random, any difference in choices between groups can be traced to the information itself.

The paper's answer

As stated in the abstract and results

Showing revenue in dollars made development options more attractive. Showing water-quality losses in dollars made people avoid polluted outcomes more strongly. Cultural heritage, which was never priced, barely registered.

If only some impacts get a dollar value, decisions may tilt toward those impacts. That matters for every cost–benefit analysis that leaves nature or culture unpriced.

    The main result

    Table 3, replicated

    Each number is a coefficient: how strongly a feature pulls someone toward an option, compared with keeping things as they are. Positive means more attractive, negative means less. Only signs and relative sizes carry meaning; the units are arbitrary. The bottom rows are the paper's question: the extra pull when a dollar figure was shown.

    Model

    Figure 2, replicated: each councillor's own “$ shown” effect

    The mixed logit gives every respondent their own coefficients. These are the respondent-level estimates from model 4 (Councillors), with dotted lines marking the middle 95% of respondents, drawn the same way as the paper's Figure 2.

    Comparing across models

    How much stronger does a preference get when a price is shown?

    Raw coefficients can't be compared across models because each model has its own scale. Dividing each “$ shown” effect by its baseline effect cancels the scale, leaving a plain percentage: how much stronger the pull (or push) becomes once a dollar figure is attached.

    Reproduce

    Every number comes from a script

    The pipeline is six short Python scripts plus an R cross-check. Running them in order rebuilds every table, figure and this page from the raw data file.

    pip install -r requirements.txt
python run_all.py              # steps 1-6, about 5 minutes (the mixed logit is the slow part)
Rscript crosscheck_clogit.R    # optional: re-estimates models 1-2 in R
